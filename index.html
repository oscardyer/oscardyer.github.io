How it works

Imagine a unit square with a quarter-circle of radius 1 drawn from its corner. If you throw a dart randomly at the square, the probability it lands inside the quarter-circle equals the area of that quarter-circle divided by the area of the square — that is, π/4 ÷ 1 = π/4.

This simulator throws thousands of random "darts" — each a point (x, y) where both coordinates are drawn uniformly between 0 and 1. A point falls inside the quarter-circle when x² + y² ≤ 1. Dividing the count of hits by the total gives an estimate of π/4. Multiply by 4 and you have your approximation of π.

The more points you throw, the closer the estimate converges to the true value. This is the Law of Large Numbers in action. The catch is that sampling noise only shrinks as 1/√n — so you need 100× more samples to gain just one extra decimal place of accuracy.

Other applications of Monte Carlo methods

The same core idea — use random sampling to approximate something hard to compute exactly — appears across nearly every quantitative field. Once you see it here with π, you'll recognise it everywhere.

📈
Finance & risk
Banks simulate thousands of possible market paths to price options, stress-test portfolios, and estimate Value at Risk — the potential loss on a bad day.
⚛️
Physics & nuclear
Particle transport through matter — neutrons, photons, electrons — is modelled by sampling random collision events at each step. The technique was pioneered for exactly this at Los Alamos in the 1940s.
🌡️
Climate modelling
Climate scientists run large ensembles of slightly perturbed simulations to quantify uncertainty in temperature projections and the likelihood of extreme weather events.
🤖
AI & game playing
Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) powers game-playing AIs like AlphaGo. It randomly simulates future game states to estimate which moves are most likely to lead to a win.
🧬
Drug discovery
Researchers simulate how molecules fold and bind to proteins by sampling from the vast space of possible shapes, making an otherwise intractable search computationally feasible.
🚀
Engineering & reliability
Aerospace engineers propagate uncertainty through design models — varying material tolerances, loads, and manufacturing errors — to estimate the probability of failure.

Frequently asked questions

Why is this called the Monte Carlo method?

The method was named after the Monte Carlo Casino in Monaco by physicists Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann while working on nuclear weapons simulations at Los Alamos in the 1940s. The casino reference reflects the method's reliance on randomness — the same chance that drives roulette wheels and card games.

How accurate is the Monte Carlo estimate of pi?

Accuracy improves slowly. With 100 samples you might get π ≈ 3.1, with 10,000 samples around 3.14, and with 1,000,000 samples around 3.1416. The error shrinks as 1/√n, so quadrupling the sample count only halves the error. It's a famously inefficient way to compute π precisely, but an excellent illustration of stochastic methods.

What is rejection sampling?

Rejection sampling is a technique for generating samples from a target distribution by drawing from a simpler distribution and accepting or rejecting each sample based on whether it meets a criterion. In this simulator, the criterion is whether the point lands inside the quarter-circle. Points that don't meet it are "rejected" — counted as outside — and their proportion tells us the area of the region.
